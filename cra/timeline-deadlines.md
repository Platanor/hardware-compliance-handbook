**Analysis of the legal deadlines and transitional provisions of the EU Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847)**

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 23 October 2024 on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements, known as the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), introduces unified mandatory cybersecurity standards for hardware and software solutions placed on the EU internal market. The regulation's scope covers all products with digital elements (PDEs) whose intended use, or reasonably foreseeable use, includes a direct or indirect logical or physical network connection.

The CRA's regulatory framework is rolled out in phases, with transitional periods ranging from 18 to 36 months. This approach is meant to give economic operators, conformity assessment bodies, and regulatory institutions the time they need to rebuild development processes, reporting infrastructure, and certification workflows.

**1. Legal basis and the regulation's adoption timeline**

The legal foundation of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 began with the European Parliament adopting its official position on 12 March 2024, followed by the Council of the EU's final approval of the text on 10 October 2024. The act was formally signed by the heads of both institutions in Strasbourg on 23 October 2024.

The legal text was published in the Official Journal of the European Union (OJ L 2024/2847) on 20 November 2024. Under EU law, the publication date marks the act's official promulgation, but it differs from the entry into force date. Under the regulation's own provisions, entry into force occurred on the twentieth day after publication — 10 December 2024. That is the date from which the legal transitional periods started running.

The practical application timeline is split into three main phases:

- Application of the Chapter IV provisions on authorising and notifying conformity assessment bodies — from 11 June 2026.

- The reporting obligations for actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents under Article 14 take effect — from 11 September 2026.

- Full application of all essential cybersecurity requirements (Annex I), technical documentation, conformity assessment procedures, and mandatory CE marking — from 11 December 2027.

**3. Detailed analysis of the implementation phases and procedural obligations**

**3.1. Preparation and internal development transformation phase (December 2024 – summer 2026)**

Since the regulation entered into force on 10 December 2024, the market has been in a 36-month adaptation period. During this phase, regulators have no legal basis to restrict the sale of goods for lack of CRA marking, but for manufacturers this window is a critical opportunity to rebuild internal engineering and operational processes.

The main task for developers during this period is to carry out a comprehensive gap analysis of current products against the Annex I requirements. Manufacturers need to build vulnerability handling processes, integrate automated software bill of materials (SBOM) generation tools into their CI/CD pipelines, and adopt threat-modelling methodology at the early design stage.

**3.2. Conformity assessment infrastructure: notified bodies and M/606 standardisation**

From 11 June 2026, the Chapter IV provisions (Articles 35–51) take effect, launching the accreditation and notification procedure for conformity assessment bodies (notified bodies) in EU Member States. This creates the legal basis for involving independent expert organisations in auditing higher-risk devices.

Class II products (hypervisors and container runtime environments, firewalls, intrusion detection and prevention systems (IDS/IPS), tamper-resistant microprocessors and microcontrollers — operating systems belong to Class I, not Class II) and Critical products (including smartcards and cryptographic elements) require mandatory third-party certification. Given the limited number of certification labs in the EU, it's important for manufacturers to start talks with notified bodies as soon as the registration process opens in June 2026.

In parallel, the M/606 standardisation mandate is being rolled out — adopted by the Commission on 3 February 2025 and accepted for execution by CEN, CENELEC, and ETSI around April 2025. The development of harmonised standards is split into three tiers:

- Horizontal Type A standards (notably EN 40000-1-2), setting out general cyber-resilience principles.

- Product-agnostic Type B standards (EN 40000-1-3, EN 40000-1-4), governing universal security requirements and vulnerability disclosure procedures under ISO/IEC 29147 and ISO/IEC 30111.

- Vertical Type C standards (the EN 304 6xx series, EN 50770), setting out detailed specifications for specific equipment categories (routers, modems, smart cameras, PLCs).

Under the current (original) M/606 timeline, the core horizontal standards (including vulnerability handling) are due by 30 August 2026, the vertical (product-specific) standards by 30 October 2026, and the remaining horizontal standards by 30 October 2027. Note that in early July 2026 the Commission published a not-yet-approved draft amendment that could push the two 2026 deadlines back by roughly two months (more detail in the "Essential requirements and standardisation" section); the 2027 deadline is unaffected by this amendment. These dates are deadlines for drafters to submit drafts to the Commission, not publication dates for the standards themselves. Manufacturers should note that the presumption of conformity under Article 27 only arises once a standard's reference is officially cited in the EU Official Journal (OJEU). If a standard is not published by the time the law fully applies, manufacturers of Class I products will be unable to rely on the simplified self-declaration regime (Article 32(2)) and will need to involve a notified body.

**3.3. Operational vulnerability and incident notification mechanism (Article 14)**

The first legally binding operational deadline for manufacturers will be **11 September 2026**. From that date, the Article 14 requirements on mandatory notification of actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents take effect.

The regulation sets out a strict three-stage timeline for notifying the competent authorities (ENISA and CSIRTs):

1. **Early warning**: sent within **24 hours** of discovering active exploitation of a vulnerability or the occurrence of an incident, indicating whether unlawful or malicious acts are suspected.

2. **Vulnerability/incident notification**: submitted within **72 hours**, including detailed information about the nature of the vulnerability, mitigation measures taken, and the sensitivity level of the information.

3. **Final report**: submitted no later than **14 days** after a corrective patch or remediation measure is released for a vulnerability, or within **1 month** of the notification in the case of a severe incident.

Reporting will go through ENISA's Single Reporting Platform, with trial access opening shortly before September 2026.

**4. Rights, exceptions, and the "grandfathering" regime for legacy products (Article 69)**

Article 69 of the regulation governs the legal status of products placed on the market before the CRA's full application date, creating a special legal-continuity regime.

**4.1. The individual-unit principle for placing on the market**

Under EU regulatory practice, the concept of "placing on the market" applies to each **individual physical or digital unit of a product**, not to an entire product line or model.

If a specific unit of a device, or a copy of software, was legally placed on the EU market (transferred into ownership or possession under an agreement) before 11 December 2027, that unit acquires "legacy product" status and is exempt from the CRA's CE-marking obligation and Annex I compliance checks. However, if new units of the same model are manufactured and sold on or after 11 December 2027, each such new unit is subject to **full compliance with the regulation's requirements**.

**4.2. The "grandfathering clause" legal mechanism and substantial modification**

Under **Article 69(2)**, products placed on the market before 11 December 2027 are subject to the CRA's essential requirements only if, after that date, they undergo a **substantial modification**.

The criteria for a substantial modification are:

- A change to the product's original intended use that was not envisaged at the time of its original release.

- A modification that affects compliance with the essential cybersecurity requirements or increases the device's/software's overall risk profile.

Developers should draw a clear line between types of released updates:

- **Minor updates**: routine bug fixes, security patches, and small optimisations are not considered substantial modifications. The product remains covered by the transitional provision.

- **Major updates**: adding new functional modules, reworking the core architecture, or changing how data is processed qualify as a substantial modification. Such a product loses its legacy status and must go through a full CRA conformity assessment.

**4.3. The retrospective derogation regime of Article 69(3)**

A key nuance of the transitional provisions is Article 69(3), which contains a direct exception to the general "grandfathering" rule.

The reporting obligations for actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents (Article 14) apply to **all products with digital elements placed on the market before 11 December 2027**, provided they are within the manufacturer's technical support phase.

This means that, starting in September 2026, the scope of mandatory monitoring and reporting covers not only new development, but the entire installed base of previously sold hardware and software released in prior years.

**4.4. Transitional period for existing conformity certificates (Article 69(1))**

EU-type examination certificates and equivalent approval decisions, issued for cybersecurity requirements under other harmonised EU legislation, remain legally valid until **11 June 2028** (unless they expire earlier). This gives manufacturers an eighteen-month buffer after the CRA's full-application date to go through re-certification under the new rules.

**5. Conclusions and strategic recommendations for developers and manufacturers**

A comprehensive analysis of the Cyber Resilience Act's regulatory timeline shows that real compliance work needs to start well before the final deadline of 11 December 2027.

The following key strategic steps have been identified for manufacturers:

1. **Product review and inventory (by September 2026)**: manufacturers need to build a complete registry of all active and supported software and hardware versions. Starting 11 September 2026, discovering any actively exploited vulnerability in supported legacy software requires immediately launching the reporting procedure to ENISA within 24 hours.

2. **Implementing Security-by-Design processes and SBOM generation**: by December 2027, companies must integrate automated software bill of materials generation (SBOM in SPDX or CycloneDX format) into development, and ensure risk documentation in line with Annex VII.

3. **Distinguishing types of software updates**: manufacturers are advised to set a clear internal-policy line between security patches (minor updates) and major functional releases (major updates), to avoid accidentally losing the benefit of the transitional regime for legacy products.

4. **Early engagement with notified bodies**: for devices falling under Class II and Critical, manufacturers should arrange conformity assessment agreements as soon as the notified-body registration lists open in June 2026, to avoid queues ahead of the regulation's full application.
