**Penalties and enforcement under the EU Cyber Resilience Act**

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 23 October 2024 on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements, known as the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), introduces unified regulatory standards for the security of software and hardware within the EU internal market. Enforcement of the regulation rests on a three-tier system of administrative financial penalties, combined with a broad set of operational market-surveillance measures. The regulatory model is built on the principle of deterrent, proportionate, and effective accountability for economic operators across the digital-product supply chain.

**1. Categories of infringement and maximum fines (Article 64 CRA)**

Article 64 of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 sets out a strict three-tier scale of administrative financial penalties. The financial ceilings are set as fixed amounts in euros or as a percentage of an economic operator's total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher.

**Grading of liability by infringement category**

The highest tier of liability applies to breaches of fundamental substantive and procedural cybersecurity rules. Under Article 64(2) of the regulation, non-compliance with the essential cybersecurity requirements set out in Annex I (in particular ensuring architectural security, protection against unauthorised access, and data integrity), as well as failure to meet manufacturers' core obligations under Article 13 and the requirements for notifying actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents under Article 14, carries administrative fines of up to EUR 15,000,000 or up to 2.5% of total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year.

The second tier of liability concerns procedural and operational breaches by various market participants. Under Article 64(3), failure by authorised representatives, importers, and distributors to meet their obligations under Articles 18–23, breach of the rules for drawing up the EU declaration of conformity under Article 28, breach of the rules and conditions for affixing the CE marking under Article 30(1)–(4), non-compliance with technical documentation requirements under Article 31, breach of the conformity assessment procedures (Modules A/B/C/H) under Article 32(1), (2), and (3), and breaches of Articles 33(5), 39, 41, 47, 49, and 53 are punishable by fines of up to EUR 10,000,000 or up to 2% of total worldwide annual turnover.

The third tier covers infringements related to procedural interaction with regulatory authorities. Under Article 64(4) of the regulation, supplying false, incomplete, or misleading information to notified bodies or market surveillance authorities in response to their official request carries a fine of up to EUR 5,000,000 or up to 1% of total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year.

| **Infringement category** | **Legal basis (CRA)** | **Fixed cap** | **Turnover cap** | **Covered operators and obligations** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Breach of essential cybersecurity requirements and reporting obligations** | Article 64(2), Annex I, Articles 13, 14 | EUR 15,000,000 | 2.5% of worldwide annual turnover | Manufacturers: failure to follow security-by-design principles, concealing vulnerabilities, absence of patch management |
| **Breach of documentation, conformity assessment, and supply-chain obligations** | Article 64(3), Articles 18–23, 28, 30–33, 39, 41, 47, 49, 53 | EUR 10,000,000 | 2.0% of worldwide annual turnover | Manufacturers, importers, distributors: missing declarations or technical files, breach of CE-marking rules |
| **Supplying unsatisfactory or misleading information to authorities** | Article 64(4) | EUR 5,000,000 | 1.0% of worldwide annual turnover | All economic operators: submitting misleading information to surveillance authorities or notified bodies |

**Assessment of circumstances and statutory exceptions**

The process of imposing fines requires competent authorities to observe the principle of individualised punishment. Article 64(5) of the regulation obliges surveillance authorities, when calculating the specific amount of a fine, to take into account the nature, duration, and gravity of the infringement, any repeat infringements within the EU, and the size of the undertaking (including micro-, small-, or medium-enterprise or start-up status) and its market share.

The regulation provides solid protections for certain categories of developers and small businesses. Under Article 64(10)(a), microenterprises and small enterprises are exempt from administrative fines for missing the initial vulnerability-notification deadlines set out in Articles 14(2)(a) and 14(4)(a). Under Article 64(10)(b), fines do not apply at all to open-source software stewards for infringements of the CRA. This is a separate mechanism from the one covering non-commercial FOSS developers — the latter are not subject to fines not because of the exemption in Article 64(10)(b), but because their activity, being outside the course of a commercial activity, places them outside the scope of the CRA altogether (Article 2(1) and Recital 18). Together, both mechanisms prevent the penalty regime from having a destructive effect on the FOSS ecosystem. Under Article 64(7), Member States separately decide for themselves whether the administrative fine regime extends to their own public authorities and public bodies.

**2. Institutional oversight and EU-level coordination**

The enforcement infrastructure of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 combines decentralised national supervision with mandatory horizontal coordination mechanisms at EU level.

**Powers of national market surveillance authorities**

Direct enforcement of the CRA and the imposition of financial and administrative penalties rests with national market surveillance authorities (MSAs), designated by each Member State under Article 52 of the regulation and the general legislative framework of Regulation (EU) 2019/1020. Under Article 64(1) and Article 64(8), each Member State determines its own national procedural rules for applying fines, which may be imposed by competent administrative authorities or by national courts, provided they have equivalent legal effect.

As part of their supervisory procedures, national MSAs have the power to require economic operators to provide technical documentation, software specifications, and risk assessment results under Article 53 of the regulation. Under Article 52(5), market surveillance authorities carrying out investigations have the procedural right to involve Computer Security Incident Response Teams (CSIRTs) or the EU Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) to obtain expert analysis of digital product compliance.

**Supranational mechanisms for market unity**

To prevent systemic divergence in enforcement between EU countries, the regulation introduces a number of coordination tools:

- **The ICSMS information system**: under Article 64(6), MSAs are required to inform their counterparts in other Member States of every instance of an administrative fine or restrictive measure through the market surveillance information and communication system (ICSMS) provided for in Article 34 of Regulation (EU) 2019/1020.

- **ENISA's Single Reporting Platform**: as part of the Article 14 obligations to notify actively exploited vulnerabilities, manufacturers submit information through the Single Reporting Platform set up by ENISA under Article 16, which gives national CSIRTs and EU bodies simultaneous access to threat data.

- **The Union safeguard procedure**: under Article 55 of the regulation, if the MSA of one country takes measures to prohibit or withdraw a digital product from the market, other Member States have three months to raise objections. If a cross-border dispute arises, or the Commission considers the measures unlawful, the Commission assesses the justification of the national MSA's action within nine months and issues a decision binding on all 27 Member States of the Union.

**3. Restrictive, corrective measures and non-financial consequences**

Regulatory enforcement under Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 covers a broad range of operational response measures that can be imposed independently of financial fines. Article 64(9) expressly provides that administrative fines apply both together with corrective or restrictive measures and separately from them.

**Range of market enforcement measures (Article 54 CRA)**

If a market surveillance authority finds that a product with digital elements does not comply with the requirements of the CRA, it requires the economic operator to take corrective action. Depending on the level of risk, the following enforcement measures apply:

- **Bringing into conformity**: an obligation on the manufacturer to fix security shortcomings, release a code patch, or supplement the technical documentation within a set time frame, without halting sales, if the threat is not critical.

- **Restriction or prohibition**: forced blocking of the commercial sale, distribution, or import of the product within the territory of the relevant Member State.

- **Withdrawal**: an enforcement measure aimed at stopping further distribution of a product that is still in the distribution network or in retailers' warehouses, but has not yet reached the end consumer.

- **Recall**: the most severe market-response measure, requiring the manufacturer to arrange for the return of a released product from end users, or its forced remote deactivation or decommissioning.

- **Mandatory public disclosure**: a requirement for the economic operator, at its own expense, to publish a warning for consumers about discovered vulnerabilities and ways to reduce risk if they continue using the product.

**Interaction with civil liability for defective products**

The application of corrective measures under the CRA is directly linked to civil-law consumer protection. If a product that is defective from a cybersecurity standpoint causes material or physical harm to users, non-compliance with the CRA's Annex I requirements becomes evidence of the product's defectiveness under the revised Product Liability Directive (PLD). This creates a risk of cumulative liability for the manufacturer: regulatory fines and product withdrawal under the CRA combine with strict damages liability in civil proceedings.

**4. Analysis of practical enforcement as of mid-2026**

The enforcement mechanism of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 operates on a clearly defined phased application timeline, set out in Article 71 of the act (Article 69 governs the narrower question of the transitional regime for products placed on the market before full application). The regulation entered into force on 10 December 2024.

As of mid-2026, there have been no instances of EU market surveillance authorities imposing administrative fines or forcibly withdrawing products from the market under Regulation (EU) 2024/2847. This is a direct result of the phased entry into force of the act's provisions:

- **11 June 2026**: the provisions of Chapter IV take effect, governing the notification and accreditation procedures for independent conformity assessment bodies (notified bodies).

- **11 September 2026**: the Article 14 obligations to promptly report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents take effect, following a three-stage timeline (24 hours for the initial early warning, 72 hours for the extended notification, and 14 days for the final report).

- **11 December 2027**: the main body of the regulation takes effect, including mandatory compliance with the essential cybersecurity requirements in Annex I, the rules for drawing up technical documentation, CE marking, and authorities' full power to impose fines under Article 64 for substantive non-compliance.

As a result, in mid-2026 the market is in a state of regulatory preparation. The activity of market surveillance authorities and the European Commission is focused on developing guidance for small and medium-sized businesses under Article 26, setting up ENISA's Single Reporting Platform, and harmonising national legal systems — not on applying punitive sanctions.

**5. Comparative analysis of the regulatory burden: CRA versus GDPR and NIS2**

To assess how strict the CRA's penalty regime is, it is useful to compare its figures with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR — Regulation (EU) 2016/679) and the Directive on measures for a high common level of cybersecurity across the Union (NIS2 — Directive (EU) 2022/2555).

| **Legal act** | **Subject matter** | **Maximum fixed fine** | **Turnover cap** | **Key subject and trigger for sanctions** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cyber Resilience Act (CRA / EU 2024/2847)** | Hardware and software with digital elements | EUR 15,000,000 | 2.5% of worldwide annual turnover | Manufacturers of digital products: releasing insecure code, concealing vulnerabilities, lack of updates |
| **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR / EU 2016/679)** | Processing and protection of natural persons' personal data | EUR 20,000,000 | 4.0% of worldwide annual turnover | Data controllers and processors: unlawful processing, personal data breaches, violation of data subject rights |
| **NIS2 Directive (EU 2022/2555)** | Entities providing critical and important infrastructure services | EUR 10,000,000 (essential) / EUR 7,000,000 (important) | 2.0% (essential) / 1.4% (important) of worldwide annual turnover | Service operators: absence of organisational cyber-hygiene measures, failure to report infrastructure disruptions |

**Comparative assessment**

The comparison reveals a deep structural logic in EU digital legislation:

- **GDPR as the absolute benchmark for severity**: the General Data Protection Regulation carries the highest financial penalty ceiling (up to 4% of worldwide turnover or EUR 20 million), reflecting its direct protection of citizens' constitutional rights and freedoms under the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights.

- **CRA as a supply-chain security priority**: the CRA sets a fine ceiling of 2.5% of worldwide turnover (or EUR 15 million), higher than the NIS2 Directive's ceiling (2% for essential entities). This reflects the EU's regulatory shift toward greater upstream security accountability for developers: fixing vulnerabilities at the product-creation stage is treated as more critical than dealing with the consequences of an attack at the operational stage.

- **Risk of cumulative sanctions**: an organisation that develops software for critical infrastructure faces a risk of triple liability if a systemic code defect is found. Non-compliance with product security requirements triggers a fine and product withdrawal under the CRA (up to 2.5% of turnover); a service outage caused by exploitation of that vulnerability triggers sanctions under NIS2 (up to 2% of turnover); and a personal-data breach resulting from the same incident opens grounds for a fine under the GDPR (up to 4% of turnover).

**6. Conclusions**

The Cyber Resilience Act (EU) 2024/2847 creates a comprehensive, strict, and harmonised enforcement system that directly combines financial penalties with strict operational restrictions on market access. The fine structure under Article 64, reaching EUR 15 million or 2.5% of worldwide turnover, places the CRA among the most consequential instruments of EU digital regulation.

While as of mid-2026 there are no practical instances of fines being imposed, due to the phased implementation timeline, the approach of September 2026 requires manufacturers and suppliers to urgently roll out vulnerability-reporting processes. The full activation of all requirements in December 2027 turns cybersecurity compliance from a purely technical task into a critical factor for preserving market access and avoiding systemic legal and financial risk for the business.
